Forms are a part of our everyday life. Whether in paper or digital format, we use forms to do our banking, to buy insurance, to get medical care, to apply for universities, to check into hotels, to apply for visas, to book flights and appointments and to talk to our doctors.

![filling different types of paper forms](/markdown-assets/31.1.filling-forms.png)

We use them to write tweets, to send emails, and invoices, and to buy and sell things online!

![filling digital forms](/markdown-assets/31.2.digital-forms.png)

In [an article](https://www.wired.com/story/form-element-modern-web-mistake/) about how the `<form>` element has created the Modern Web, writer and programmer Paul Ford says that:

> **It is the `<form>` element, and the lesser elements that comprise the form, like `<input>` and `<textarea>`, that let you put little text boxes and credit card numbers and password fields into the page, along with a variety of drop-downs and checkboxes.**
>
> **I have argued many times, to the despair of anyone within range, that the `<form>` element was a pivot point for the entire technology industry. It is what changed the web from a read-only medium for physics papers into a read-write medium for anything.**

Forms enable transactions. Transactions of all kind — social and commercial. That is why **forms remain one of the most important parts of any website or application,** and one of the most important user interactions on the Web. When someone visits a website, a lot of times the only thing standing between them and accomplishing their goals is a form.

This is why it is especially important that forms don’t create a barrier to access for people. Forms are a means to an end, and users should be able to complete them efficiently and without confusion.

Now imagine you’re trying to fill out a form online and the form looks like this: a group of boxes—input fields—that have no labels.

![A signup forms with two input fields, a select dropdown, and a checkbox, all missing labels that identify what they are.](/markdown-assets/31.3.inaccessible-form.webp)

If you’re filling out a paper form, it might look like this:

![A paper application form requesting personal details as a first step in the application. The application form contains a bunch of input fields with no labels.](/markdown-assets/31.4.inaccessible-paper-form.webp)

You’re expected to fill out a form but the form fields have no labels to identify them. You know you’re expected to fill _something_ in, but you can’t identify what that thing is.

Unfortunately, this is how many users of assistive technologies experience forms on the Web today.

According to [the WebAIM 2023](https://webaim.org/projects/million/) report on the accessibility of the top 1,000,000 home pages: **“35.8% of the 4.4 million to 5 million form inputs identified were not properly labeled”.**

WCAG [S**uccess Criterion 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions** (Level A)](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#labels-or-instructions) states that:

> **Labels or instructions are provided when content requires user input.**

- Providing clear and unambiguous labels and instructions (including examples of expected data formats) helps all users, but particularly those with cognitive, language, and learning disabilities - to enter information correctly.

- Providing clear and unambiguous labels and instructions (including clear identification of required fields) can prevent users from making incomplete or incorrect form submissions, which prevents them from having to navigate once more through a page/form in order to fix submission errors.

In this chapter, we’re going to learn how to properly label and identify form controls, how to provide instructions when they are needed, as well as further improve the usability of forms using helpful native HTML attributes.

## Identifying form controls using the `label` element

Forms typically include labels that are visually associated with their controls. Sighted users perceive structure and relationships through various visual cues. Layout, positioning and spacing create a visual association that helps the user identify a control by its label.

![properly labelled forms](/markdown-assets/31.5.proper-digital-form.png)

But, as you know by now, blind screen reader users rely on accessible names to identify controls. And **a label is not an accessible name unless it is programmatically associated with its control.**

WCAG **SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)** states that:

> **For all user interface components (including but not limited to: form elements, links and components generated by scripts), the name and role can be programmatically determined;**

**SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)** states that:

> **Information, structure, and relationships conveyed through presentation can be programmatically determined or are available in text.**

This means that **the relationship between the label and the input field that is conveyed by their visual styles must also be conveyed programmatically.**

To provide an accessible name to most form controls you can use the `<label>` element, and associate the label with its control using the for and id attributes. The for attribute on the `<label>` element needs to have the same value as the id attribute of the control it is providing the label for.

```html
<!-- This <label> provides an accName for the input -->
<label for="[ input_ID ]"
  >[ the input's label ]</label
>
<input id="[ input_ID ]" type="text" />
```

This is really important because **without these two matching attribute values, the browser won’t expose the `<label>` as the name for the `<input>`,** and when the screen reader queries the browser for this information, it won’t get it.

If you pull up the accessibility tree and inspect the accessible name for this input, you’ll see that the browser has exposed the text of the label as the accessible name for the input using the `for/id` attributes.

The problem with too many forms today is that the labels are not programmatically associated with their controls. This happens when you write code like this:

```html
<div>A div</div>
<input type="email" />
```

or this:

```html
<span>Your label</span> <input type="text" />
```

or maybe like this:

```html
<label>This is a label</label>
<input type="password" />
```

Even when you use the `<label>` element, **the label is not used as an accessible name for the input unless it is programmatically associated with it.** This association happens via the for and id attributes.

**The for attribute only works on the `<label>` element.** So the following markup is not valid, and it does not create a programmatic association between the visual label and its input:

```html
<!-- This is not valid. -->
<div for="username">Your label</div>
<input id="username" type="text" />
```

We will talk about how to fix code like this in a few minutes.

Using the for and `id` attributes creates an _explicit_ association between the label and its control. You can also create an _implicit_ association by placing the input _inside_ the `<label>`:

```html
<!-- This creates an implicit association between the label and its control -->
<label>
  [ the input's label ]
  <input type="text" />
</label>
```

However, **not all assistive technologies currently announce the label as the accessible name for the input when you create an implicit association between them**, so it is recommended that you **make the association explicit using the for and id attributes even if you use this markup pattern.**

```html
<!-- Create an explicit association between the label and its control for better AT support -->
<label for="username">
  [ the input's label ]
  <input id="username" type="text" />
</label>
```

The `<label>` can contain only one labelable element at a time (the one it is providing the label for). Any additional labelable elements are not allowed as they would not be labelled by that label:

```html
<label for="name">
  Your name
  <input type="text" id="name" />
  <!-- not allowed -->
  <input type="text" id="lastName" />
</label>
```

### Not all form controls are labelable

Not all form controls are labelable. Meaning that: not all form controls can get their accessible name from the `<label>` element. There is a limited set of [labelable elements](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/forms.html#labelable-element) in HTML:

- `<button>`
- `<input>` (unless it has `type="hidden"`, which should have no accessible name or label anyway since it is never presented to the user)
- `<meter>`
- `<output>`
- `<progress>`
- `<select>`
- `<textarea>`

Unless overridden by ARIA attributes, these elements get their accName natively from the `<label>` element when it is present and properly associated with them.

Other form controls such as `<input type="[ button | submit | reset ]">` get their accName natively from the value attribute, as we mentioned in [the accessible name computation chapter.](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/accName-computation/)

## Providing accessible names to form controls using ARIA

The `<label>` element is the most robust way to provide accessible names to labelable form controls. If you _can’t_ use a `<label>` element, you may use `aria-labelledby` or `aria-label`.

If the label of the input is already present somewhere in the markup, you can associate it with its input using the `aria-labelledby` attribute. This is particularly useful for when the label is created using a `<div>` or a `<span>` and you can’t change the markup to replace them with a `<label>`. Give the text label a unique ID and reference that ID in the `aria-labelledby` attribute on the input:

```html
<span id="input-label">Your name</span>
<input
  type="text"
  aria-labelledby="input-label" />

<div id="email-label">Your email</div>
<input
  type="email"
  aria-labelledby="email-label" />
```

If the label is not in already the markup and you cannot provide one, you may use `aria-label` on the control:

```html
<!-- Use aria-label only if the label is not in the markup and if you can't provide one. -->
<input type="text" aria-label="Your name" />

<input type="email" aria-label="Your email" />
```

Use `aria-labelledby` and avoid using `aria-label` when the label is present somewhere in the markup. If the label _is_ in the markup and you use `aria-label` instead of `aria-labelledby`, you need to make sure that the text label and the value of `aria-label` match. If they don’t:

- when a sighted screen reader user focuses on the control, they will hear an accessible name that does not match the label they see on the screen;

- and voice control users will not be able to activate the control usings its label, because the label they speak does not match the accessible name of the control. In this case, you also run the risk of violating WCAG [SC 2.5.3 Label in Name.](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/quickref/#label-in-name)

## Providing visually-hidden labels

Form controls should have visible labels. When a form control’s label is not visible, users with cognitive disabilities or short memory problems may find it difficult to understand or remember what they are supposed to fill in each field.

So, avoid hiding form control labels.

That being said, there might be instances of common design patterns where a control’s purpose is evident from the context that it is used in. In that case, hiding the control’s label visually might be acceptable as it wouldn’t degrade the usability of the pattern. A practical example of that is a search component that contains a search input field and a search button:

The search input field’s label may be visually hidden in this case because the context it is in (which is the search form), as well as the label of the search button sitting next to it, provide sufficient visual cues as to what the purpose of the input field is. But **the input still requires an accessible name.**

```html
<form ... role="search">
  <!-- this input needs an accName -->
  <input type="search" inputmode="search" />
  <!-- this button can also be an icon button -->
  <button type="submit">Search</button>
</form>
```

There are three **reliable ways you may provide an accessible name** to an input that has a visually-hidden label. We will use the search input field in this example to demonstrate the three approaches.

### Using a visually-hidden label

You can start by providing a `<label>` for the input and visually hiding it using the visually-hidden utility class (that we learned all about in [the hiding techniques chapter](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/hiding-techniques/)):

```html
<label for="search" class="visually-hidden"
  >Search</label
>
<input
  type="search"
  id="search"
  inputmode="search" />
```

I love this approach because not only does it not require the use of ARIA, but is is also resilient — if your CSS is not used, the label is shown by default, which is always a usability win.

### Using `aria-labelledby`

You can also use the `aria-labelledby` attribute to reference a visually-hidden text node (one that is hidden using the HTML `hidden` attribute):

```html
<span for="search" id="search-input-label" hidden
  >Search</span
>
<input
  type="search"
  id="search"
  inputmode="search"
  aria-labelledby="search-input-label" />
```

…or to reference an element that is already present in the markup. For a search form, this is particularly handy.

When you reference the ID of an element in `aria-labelledby,` the computed text of that element will be used as the accessible name of the input.

The accessible name for the input field is “Search”. **And so is the accessible name of the button sitting next to it.** So we can use the button to provide an accessible name for the input:

```html
<input
  type="search"
  aria-labelledby="search_button"
  inputmode="search" />
<button type="submit" id="search_button">
  Search
</button>
```

This is my favorite way to provide the accName for the input field in this pattern.

### Using aria-label

And lastly, you may also provide a `visually-hidden` label using `aria-label`.

```html
<input type="search" aria-label="Search" />
```

Keep in mind that the value of `aria-label` does not translate well. As I mentioned in the accName computation chapter, the `aria-label` attribute is at the bottom of [my order of priority for providing accessible names to elements.](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/accName-computation/#order-of-priority-for-choosing-an-accessible-name) So, I recommend choosing one of the previous two methods whenever you can.

## Don’t use the `placeholder` attribute

According to the accessible name and description computation algorithm, the placeholder attribute’s value, when present, should be used as an accessible name for the form control it is used on, when none of the other methods are provided. But you should generally avoid using the `placeholder` attribute.

The `placeholder` attribute is used to provide placeholder text for input fields that can have one.

Placeholder text is **text that, when present, appears in the form control when it has no value set.**

```html
<label for="name">Full Name</label>
<input
  id="name"
  type="text"
  placeholder="e.g. Jane Doe" />
```

To save on vertical space on smaller screens, and for pure aesthetic reasons otherwise, many designers will substitute placeholder text for visible labels, or use placeholders to provide hints or instructions for input requirements.

![using placeholder in forms](/markdown-assets/31.6.placeholder-example.png)

Using placeholders as a replacement for labels creates a serious accessibility and usability problem. _"The placeholder attribute contains a surprising amount of issues that prevent it from delivering on what it promises"_, says inclusive designer Eric Bailey in his article [“Don’t use the placeholder attribute”.](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2018/06/placeholder-attribute/)

Eric’s article is my go-to reference for providing an elaborate argument against using the placeholder attribute. I cannot recommend it enough. I’m going to highlight some of the most important points in his article:

- Like `aria-label`, the value of the `placeholder` attribute does not translate well. Browsers with auto-translation features such as Chrome skip over attributes when a request to translate the current page is initiated.

> **If a person is requesting a page to be translated, the expectation is that all visible page content will be updated. Placeholders are frequently used to provide important input formatting instructions or are used in place of a more appropriate label element (more on that in a bit). If this content is not updated along with the rest of the translated page, there is a high possibility that a person unfamiliar with the language will not be able to successfully understand and operate the input.**

- Placeholder text creates usability barriers for people with cognitive disabilities.

"Cognitive disability (also known as intellectual disability) is a term that describes a person who has greater difficulty with mental tasks than the average person." ([“Cognitive”](https://webaim.org/articles/cognitive/), WebAIM)

People with cognitive disabilities include people with short-term memory loss, traumatic brain injury, and Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). **Cognitive disabilities are by far the most common type of disability.**

> **Cognition can be inhibited situational, meaning it can very well happen to you. It can be affected by things like multitasking, sleep deprivation, stress, substance abuse, and depression.**

When a user enters information into a text field, the placeholder content disappears. This means that as soon as they start typing into the field, the instructions that they need to help them fill the field are removed from sight. And the only way they can restore that information is by deleting the information that they have entered.
