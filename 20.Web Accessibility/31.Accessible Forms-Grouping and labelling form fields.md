Forms are a part of our everyday life. Whether in paper or digital format, we use forms to do our banking, to buy insurance, to get medical care, to apply for universities, to check into hotels, to apply for visas, to book flights and appointments and to talk to our doctors.

![filling different types of paper forms](/markdown-assets/31.1.filling-forms.png)

We use them to write tweets, to send emails, and invoices, and to buy and sell things online!

![filling digital forms](/markdown-assets/31.2.digital-forms.png)

In [an article](https://www.wired.com/story/form-element-modern-web-mistake/) about how the `<form>` element has created the Modern Web, writer and programmer Paul Ford says that:

> **It is the `<form>` element, and the lesser elements that comprise the form, like `<input>` and `<textarea>`, that let you put little text boxes and credit card numbers and password fields into the page, along with a variety of drop-downs and checkboxes.**
>
> **I have argued many times, to the despair of anyone within range, that the `<form>` element was a pivot point for the entire technology industry. It is what changed the web from a read-only medium for physics papers into a read-write medium for anything.**

Forms enable transactions. Transactions of all kind — social and commercial. That is why **forms remain one of the most important parts of any website or application,** and one of the most important user interactions on the Web. When someone visits a website, a lot of times the only thing standing between them and accomplishing their goals is a form.

This is why it is especially important that forms don’t create a barrier to access for people. Forms are a means to an end, and users should be able to complete them efficiently and without confusion.

Now imagine you’re trying to fill out a form online and the form looks like this: a group of boxes—input fields—that have no labels.

![A signup forms with two input fields, a select dropdown, and a checkbox, all missing labels that identify what they are.](/markdown-assets/31.3.inaccessible-form.webp)

If you’re filling out a paper form, it might look like this:

![A paper application form requesting personal details as a first step in the application. The application form contains a bunch of input fields with no labels.](/markdown-assets/31.4.inaccessible-paper-form.webp)

You’re expected to fill out a form but the form fields have no labels to identify them. You know you’re expected to fill _something_ in, but you can’t identify what that thing is.

Unfortunately, this is how many users of assistive technologies experience forms on the Web today.

According to [the WebAIM 2023](https://webaim.org/projects/million/) report on the accessibility of the top 1,000,000 home pages: **“35.8% of the 4.4 million to 5 million form inputs identified were not properly labeled”.**

WCAG [S**uccess Criterion 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions** (Level A)](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#labels-or-instructions) states that:

> **Labels or instructions are provided when content requires user input.**

- Providing clear and unambiguous labels and instructions (including examples of expected data formats) helps all users, but particularly those with cognitive, language, and learning disabilities - to enter information correctly.

- Providing clear and unambiguous labels and instructions (including clear identification of required fields) can prevent users from making incomplete or incorrect form submissions, which prevents them from having to navigate once more through a page/form in order to fix submission errors.

In this chapter, we’re going to learn how to properly label and identify form controls, how to provide instructions when they are needed, as well as further improve the usability of forms using helpful native HTML attributes.

## Identifying form controls using the `label` element

Forms typically include labels that are visually associated with their controls. Sighted users perceive structure and relationships through various visual cues. Layout, positioning and spacing create a visual association that helps the user identify a control by its label.

![properly labelled forms](/markdown-assets/31.5.proper-digital-form.png)

But, as you know by now, blind screen reader users rely on accessible names to identify controls. And **a label is not an accessible name unless it is programmatically associated with its control.**

WCAG **SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)** states that:

> **For all user interface components (including but not limited to: form elements, links and components generated by scripts), the name and role can be programmatically determined;**

**SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)** states that:

> **Information, structure, and relationships conveyed through presentation can be programmatically determined or are available in text.**

This means that **the relationship between the label and the input field that is conveyed by their visual styles must also be conveyed programmatically.**

To provide an accessible name to most form controls you can use the `<label>` element, and associate the label with its control using the for and id attributes. The for attribute on the `<label>` element needs to have the same value as the id attribute of the control it is providing the label for.

```html
<!-- This <label> provides an accName for the input -->
<label for="[ input_ID ]"
  >[ the input's label ]</label
>
<input id="[ input_ID ]" type="text" />
```

This is really important because **without these two matching attribute values, the browser won’t expose the `<label>` as the name for the `<input>`,** and when the screen reader queries the browser for this information, it won’t get it.

If you pull up the accessibility tree and inspect the accessible name for this input, you’ll see that the browser has exposed the text of the label as the accessible name for the input using the `for/id` attributes.

The problem with too many forms today is that the labels are not programmatically associated with their controls. This happens when you write code like this:

```html
<div>A div</div>
<input type="email" />
```

or this:

```html
<span>Your label</span> <input type="text" />
```

or maybe like this:

```html
<label>This is a label</label>
<input type="password" />
```

Even when you use the `<label>` element, **the label is not used as an accessible name for the input unless it is programmatically associated with it.** This association happens via the for and id attributes.

**The for attribute only works on the `<label>` element.** So the following markup is not valid, and it does not create a programmatic association between the visual label and its input:

```html
<!-- This is not valid. -->
<div for="username">Your label</div>
<input id="username" type="text" />
```

We will talk about how to fix code like this in a few minutes.

Using the for and `id` attributes creates an _explicit_ association between the label and its control. You can also create an _implicit_ association by placing the input _inside_ the `<label>`:

```html
<!-- This creates an implicit association between the label and its control -->
<label>
  [ the input's label ]
  <input type="text" />
</label>
```

However, **not all assistive technologies currently announce the label as the accessible name for the input when you create an implicit association between them**, so it is recommended that you **make the association explicit using the for and id attributes even if you use this markup pattern.**

```html
<!-- Create an explicit association between the label and its control for better AT support -->
<label for="username">
  [ the input's label ]
  <input id="username" type="text" />
</label>
```

The `<label>` can contain only one labelable element at a time (the one it is providing the label for). Any additional labelable elements are not allowed as they would not be labelled by that label:

```html
<label for="name">
  Your name
  <input type="text" id="name" />
  <!-- not allowed -->
  <input type="text" id="lastName" />
</label>
```

### Not all form controls are labelable

Not all form controls are labelable. Meaning that: not all form controls can get their accessible name from the `<label>` element. There is a limited set of [labelable elements](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/forms.html#labelable-element) in HTML:

- `<button>`
- `<input>` (unless it has `type="hidden"`, which should have no accessible name or label anyway since it is never presented to the user)
- `<meter>`
- `<output>`
- `<progress>`
- `<select>`
- `<textarea>`

Unless overridden by ARIA attributes, these elements get their accName natively from the `<label>` element when it is present and properly associated with them.

Other form controls such as `<input type="[ button | submit | reset ]">` get their accName natively from the value attribute, as we mentioned in [the accessible name computation chapter.](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/accName-computation/)

## Providing accessible names to form controls using ARIA

The `<label>` element is the most robust way to provide accessible names to labelable form controls. If you _can’t_ use a `<label>` element, you may use `aria-labelledby` or `aria-label`.

If the label of the input is already present somewhere in the markup, you can associate it with its input using the `aria-labelledby` attribute. This is particularly useful for when the label is created using a `<div>` or a `<span>` and you can’t change the markup to replace them with a `<label>`. Give the text label a unique ID and reference that ID in the `aria-labelledby` attribute on the input:

```html
<span id="input-label">Your name</span>
<input
  type="text"
  aria-labelledby="input-label" />

<div id="email-label">Your email</div>
<input
  type="email"
  aria-labelledby="email-label" />
```

If the label is not in already the markup and you cannot provide one, you may use `aria-label` on the control:

```html
<!-- Use aria-label only if the label is not in the markup and if you can't provide one. -->
<input type="text" aria-label="Your name" />

<input type="email" aria-label="Your email" />
```

Use `aria-labelledby` and avoid using `aria-label` when the label is present somewhere in the markup. If the label _is_ in the markup and you use `aria-label` instead of `aria-labelledby`, you need to make sure that the text label and the value of `aria-label` match. If they don’t:

- when a sighted screen reader user focuses on the control, they will hear an accessible name that does not match the label they see on the screen;

- and voice control users will not be able to activate the control usings its label, because the label they speak does not match the accessible name of the control. In this case, you also run the risk of violating WCAG [SC 2.5.3 Label in Name.](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/quickref/#label-in-name)

## Providing visually-hidden labels

Form controls should have visible labels. When a form control’s label is not visible, users with cognitive disabilities or short memory problems may find it difficult to understand or remember what they are supposed to fill in each field.

So, avoid hiding form control labels.

That being said, there might be instances of common design patterns where a control’s purpose is evident from the context that it is used in. In that case, hiding the control’s label visually might be acceptable as it wouldn’t degrade the usability of the pattern. A practical example of that is a search component that contains a search input field and a search button:

The search input field’s label may be visually hidden in this case because the context it is in (which is the search form), as well as the label of the search button sitting next to it, provide sufficient visual cues as to what the purpose of the input field is. But **the input still requires an accessible name.**

```html
<form ... role="search">
  <!-- this input needs an accName -->
  <input type="search" inputmode="search" />
  <!-- this button can also be an icon button -->
  <button type="submit">Search</button>
</form>
```

There are three **reliable ways you may provide an accessible name** to an input that has a visually-hidden label. We will use the search input field in this example to demonstrate the three approaches.

### Using a visually-hidden label

You can start by providing a `<label>` for the input and visually hiding it using the visually-hidden utility class (that we learned all about in [the hiding techniques chapter](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/hiding-techniques/)):

```html
<label for="search" class="visually-hidden"
  >Search</label
>
<input
  type="search"
  id="search"
  inputmode="search" />
```

I love this approach because not only does it not require the use of ARIA, but is is also resilient — if your CSS is not used, the label is shown by default, which is always a usability win.

### Using `aria-labelledby`

You can also use the `aria-labelledby` attribute to reference a visually-hidden text node (one that is hidden using the HTML `hidden` attribute):

```html
<span for="search" id="search-input-label" hidden
  >Search</span
>
<input
  type="search"
  id="search"
  inputmode="search"
  aria-labelledby="search-input-label" />
```

…or to reference an element that is already present in the markup. For a search form, this is particularly handy.

When you reference the ID of an element in `aria-labelledby,` the computed text of that element will be used as the accessible name of the input.

The accessible name for the input field is “Search”. **And so is the accessible name of the button sitting next to it.** So we can use the button to provide an accessible name for the input:

```html
<input
  type="search"
  aria-labelledby="search_button"
  inputmode="search" />
<button type="submit" id="search_button">
  Search
</button>
```

This is my favorite way to provide the accName for the input field in this pattern.

### Using aria-label

And lastly, you may also provide a `visually-hidden` label using `aria-label`.

```html
<input type="search" aria-label="Search" />
```

Keep in mind that the value of `aria-label` does not translate well. As I mentioned in the accName computation chapter, the `aria-label` attribute is at the bottom of [my order of priority for providing accessible names to elements.](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/accName-computation/#order-of-priority-for-choosing-an-accessible-name) So, I recommend choosing one of the previous two methods whenever you can.

## Don’t use the `placeholder` attribute

According to the accessible name and description computation algorithm, the placeholder attribute’s value, when present, should be used as an accessible name for the form control it is used on, when none of the other methods are provided. But you should generally avoid using the `placeholder` attribute.

The `placeholder` attribute is used to provide placeholder text for input fields that can have one.

Placeholder text is **text that, when present, appears in the form control when it has no value set.**

```html
<label for="name">Full Name</label>
<input
  id="name"
  type="text"
  placeholder="e.g. Jane Doe" />
```

To save on vertical space on smaller screens, and for pure aesthetic reasons otherwise, many designers will substitute placeholder text for visible labels, or use placeholders to provide hints or instructions for input requirements.

![using placeholder in forms](/markdown-assets/31.6.placeholder-example.png)

Using placeholders as a replacement for labels creates a serious accessibility and usability problem. _"The placeholder attribute contains a surprising amount of issues that prevent it from delivering on what it promises"_, says inclusive designer Eric Bailey in his article [“Don’t use the placeholder attribute”.](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2018/06/placeholder-attribute/)

Eric’s article is my go-to reference for providing an elaborate argument against using the placeholder attribute. I cannot recommend it enough. I’m going to highlight some of the most important points in his article:

- Like `aria-label`, the value of the `placeholder` attribute does not translate well. Browsers with auto-translation features such as Chrome skip over attributes when a request to translate the current page is initiated.

> **If a person is requesting a page to be translated, the expectation is that all visible page content will be updated. Placeholders are frequently used to provide important input formatting instructions or are used in place of a more appropriate label element (more on that in a bit). If this content is not updated along with the rest of the translated page, there is a high possibility that a person unfamiliar with the language will not be able to successfully understand and operate the input.**

- Placeholder text creates usability barriers for people with cognitive disabilities.

"Cognitive disability (also known as intellectual disability) is a term that describes a person who has greater difficulty with mental tasks than the average person." ([“Cognitive”](https://webaim.org/articles/cognitive/), WebAIM)

People with cognitive disabilities include people with short-term memory loss, traumatic brain injury, and Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). **Cognitive disabilities are by far the most common type of disability.**

> **Cognition can be inhibited situational, meaning it can very well happen to you. It can be affected by things like multitasking, sleep deprivation, stress, substance abuse, and depression.**

When a user enters information into a text field, the placeholder content disappears. This means that as soon as they start typing into the field, the instructions that they need to help them fill the field are removed from sight. And the only way they can restore that information is by deleting the information that they have entered.

[password placeholder](/markdown-assets/31.7.password-placeholder.gif)
_Wait—what’s the minimum length? How many numbers do they want again? (Image source: Smashing Magazine)_

WCAG SC 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions (Level A) states that _"labels or instructions are provided when content requires user input."_

Because the placeholder label disappears once the user starts typing into a field—i.e. it disappears precisely when the user needs it the most, using placeholder as a label is a failure of this success criterion.

_The placeholder-label will also not be visible when the form is shown to the user with the fields already filled in, and that is also a failure of the success criterion._

Browsers typically [style placeholder text](https://cdpn.io/pen/debug/dKbbEV) using a light grey color to differentiate it from normal text in and around the input field. This color has a low contrast ratio by default that often fails WCAG minimum color contrast ratio requirements.

> To recap, the placeholder attribute:
>
> - Can’t be automatically translated;
>
> - Is oftentimes used in place of a label, locking out assistive technology;
>
> - Can hide important information when content is entered;
>
> - Can be too light-colored to be legible;
>
> - Has limited styling options;
>
> - May look like pre-filled information and be skipped over.

For all of these reasons, you should avoid using the `placeholder` attribute.

To give your input fields an accessible name, use text in a `<label>`, or use ARIA attributes if you have to.

## Provide additional instructions outside the label

Some form controls such as password and email fields often come with instructions (such as formatting hints, warnings, or error messages) that help the user fill information in.

![filed with description](/markdown-assets/31.9.field-description-text.png)

The accessible name should be short and succinct. When you need to provide hints or additional instructions that help the user fill information in form fields, provide these instructions outside the `<label>`, and associate them with the input using `aria-describedby`.

For example:

```html
<label for="passw0rd">Create a password</label>
<input
  type="password"
  id="passw0rd"
  aria-describedby="passw0rd-requirements" />
<div id="passw0rd-requirements">
  <p>You password should be:</p>
  <ul>
    <li>
      At least 12 characters — the more
      characters, the better.
    </li>
    <li>
      A mixture of both uppercase and lowercase
      letters.
    </li>
    <li>A mixture of letters and numbers.</li>
    <li>
      Including at least one special character,
      e.g., ! @ # ? ]
    </li>
  </ul>
</div>
```

Hints and instructions may be provided as a short sentence, or in a longer more structured format (such as _a list_ of requirements).

As we mentioned in [the accessible names and descriptions chapter,](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/accName/) the descriptive content referenced in `aria-describedby` is flattened to a string of text, so most screen readers will not expose the structure in the descriptive text provided in this example.

Instructions associated with the input using `aria-describedby` are appended, as descriptive text, after the accessible name and role of an element have been announced by screen readers. Screen readers will typically pause after the name and role of the control have been announced, and before they announce the accessible description. This creates a more elegant experience than announcing both the name and description as one piece of text.

## Grouping related form controls together

Providing a name to each individual form control is critical. But there are times where a control or group of controls may need more context to help the user identify their purpose. _Grouping controls_ and giving the group an accessible name may be just the context they need.

For example, say you have a group of radio buttons each representing a different color that the user is asked to choose from. The user can identify the radio buttons by their individual names (black, purple, green, etc.). But the individual button names are not enough to identify the purpose of these options — i.e. what the user is choosing a color for. Grouping these options and giving the group a label will identify the purpose of the group and help the user understand what they are choosing a color for.

```html
<!-- what do these colors represent? What is the user choosing a color for? -->
<label for="black"
  ><input
    type="radio"
    name="color"
    value="black"
    id="black" />Black</label
>
<label for="purple"
  ><input
    type="radio"
    name="color"
    value="purple"
    id="purple" />Purple</label
>
<label for="green"
  ><input
    type="radio"
    name="color"
    value="green"
    id="green" />Green</label
>
```

![grouping-radio-buttons](/markdown-assets/31.10.grouping-radio-buttons.png)

Another instance where grouping related controls is necessary is when you have two groups of identical fields that serve a different purpose.

For example, an e-commerce website will typically ask the user for two addresses: a shipping address and a billing address. Both the shipping address and the billing address require the same information (and therefore, the same input fields). The fields also share the same names. It is the group label for each type of address that provides the context and identifies the purpose of each input field within that group.

![Two grounds of form fields: one group is labelled ‘Shipping Address’, and the other group is labelled ‘Billing address’. Both groups have the same fields: name, street, apartment number, city, and zip code.](/markdown-assets/31.11.grouping-fields-using-fieldset.png)

Grouping controls is also useful for presenting multiple choice questions, for example. The group label identifies the question that the answers are in response to.

### Grouping controls using `fieldset` and `legend`

To group form controls together, you should use the native HTML `<fieldset>` element.

> **The fieldset element represents a set of form controls (or other content) grouped together, optionally with a caption. The caption is given by the first legend element that is a child of the fieldset element, if any. The remainder of the descendants form the group.**
>
> — [**The HTML specification**](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/form-elements.html#the-fieldset-element)

The `<fieldset>` element has an implicit `role="group"`. And it gets its accessible name from the first `<legend>` element that is a direct child of the `<fieldset>`.

For example, here are our radio buttons grouped together in a `<fieldset>`, and given an accessible name using `<legend>`:

```html
<fieldset>
  <legend>Choose a T-shirt color:</legend>

  <label for="black"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="black"
      id="black" />Black</label
  >
  <label for="purple"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="purple"
      id="purple" />Purple</label
  >
  <label for="green"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="green"
      id="green" />Green</label
  >
</fieldset>
```

Note that `<legend>` needs to be a direct child of the `<fieldset>`, which means that the following markup breaks the relationship between them, and the browser will thus no longer expose the `<legend>` as an accessible name for the `<fieldset>`:

```html
<fieldset>
  <!-- Don’t do this -->
  <div>
    <legend>Choose a theme:</legend>
  </div>
  <label for="black"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="black"
      id="black" />Black</label
  >
  <label for="purple"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="purple"
      id="purple" />Purple</label
  >
  <label for="green"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="green"
      id="green" />Green</label
  >
</fieldset>
```

Note that, when radio buttons are identified as a group with the same name attribute, screen readers announce how many options the group contains. This is just as helpful to the user as lists (`<ul>`, `<ol>`) announcing how many items they contain.

Here is our shipping and billing address form fields also grouped using `<fieldset>`s, and labelled using `<legend>`s:

```html
<!-- example borrowed from https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/grouping/ -->
<fieldset>
  <legend>Shipping Address:</legend>
  <div>
    <label for="shipping_name">Name:</label>
    <input
      type="text"
      name="shipping_name"
      id="shipping_name" />
  </div>
  <div>
    <label for="shipping_street">Street:</label>
    <input
      type="text"
      name="shipping_street"
      id="shipping_street" />
  </div>
  […]
</fieldset>
<fieldset>
  <legend>Billing Address:</legend>
  <div>
    <label for="billing_name">Name:</label>
    <input
      type="text"
      name="billing_name"
      id="billing_name" />
  </div>
  <div>
    <label for="billing_street">Street:</label>
    <input
      type="text"
      name="billing_street"
      id="billing_street" />
  </div>
  […]
</fieldset>
```

When a `<fieldset>` and `<legend>` are used to group and label controls, screen readers typically announce them in a predictable way, indicating the start and the end of the group, as well as its name.

Using the `disabled` attribute on the `<fieldset>`, you can disable all the form controls contained within the group, **except any controls that are that are descendants of the first `<legend>` element child,** if any.

Like many other HTML elements, as we mentioned in [the accessible name computation chapter](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/accName-computation/), you can also give the `<fieldset>` an accessible name using ARIA.

Using `aria-labelledby`, you can use an element that already exists on the page as a label for the group. For example, if the group does not have a caption but is preceded by a heading that can be used as a label for the group:

```html
<h3 id="movement">
  What kind of movement do you incorporate into
  your work day?
</h3>

<fieldset aria-labelledby="movement">
  <input type="checkbox" id="yoga" />
  <label for="yoga">I do yoga</label>

  <input type="checkbox" id="pilates" />
  <label for="pilates">I do pilates</label>

  <input type="checkbox" id="walk" />
  <label for="walk"
    >I go for a 30 minute walk every
    morning</label
  >

  <input type="checkbox" id="running" />
  <label for="running">I run</label>

  ...
</fieldset>
```

If you need to, you may also use aria-label to give the `<fieldset>` an accessible name. Since groups of controls should typically be preceded by a visual label that identifies their purpose, if you use aria-label, make sure that the value of the label you provide matches the label that is visible on screen. And keep in mind that a screen reader user may then hear the same label twice.

### Grouping controls using ARIA

If you can’t use the `<fieldset>` and `<legend>` to group form controls, you can use ARIA to create a group of controls and provide an accessible name to the group using aria-labelledby.

Here is our group of radio buttons again, but this time grouped together using a `<div>` promoted to a group with ARIA, and given an accessible name using aria-labelledby:

```html
<div role="group" aria-labelledby="theme_label">
  <h3 id="theme_label">
    Choose a T-shirt color:
  </h3>

  <label for="black"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="black"
      id="black" />Black</label
  >
  <label for="purple"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="purple"
      id="purple" />Purple</label
  >
  <label for="green"
    ><input
      type="radio"
      name="color"
      value="green"
      id="green" />Green</label
  >
</div>
```

As you know from the [rules of ARIA use chapter,](https://practical-accessibility.today/chapters/aria-rules/) using ARIA should not be your default strategy. The first rule of ARIA is to avoid using ARIA if you can use native HTML to provide the semantics you need instead. This rule doesn’t exist for purely idealist reasons, it has practical reasons too; and grouping controls is an example of that.

In [an article about the Fieldset and Legend elements](https://adrianroselli.com/2022/07/use-legend-and-fieldset.html), Adrian Roselli notes that swapping to an ARIA group role will result in a more inaccessible experience. In his article, Adrian shares the results of testing the `group / aria-labelledby` construct compared to using `<fieldset> / <legend>` across screen readers on different desktop and mobile platforms. The results of his tests showed that the ARIA grouping construct was broken for TalkBack and VoiceOver users on Android and iOS. The reason is that `role="group"` support is currently not good on both Android and iOS.

So **even though ARIA provides semantic parity with HTML, it does not guarantee equal support.**

Over many years now, developers have avoided using `<fieldset>` and `<legend>` merely for visual styling reasons. Frustrating layout challenges such as CSS Grid not working as expected on `<fieldset>` and `<legend>` have had developers reaching for `<div>`s to group form controls. But you should **avoid using ARIA as a replacement for `<fieldset>` and `<legend>`** if you can.

Use `<fieldset>` and `<legend>`. If you’re worried about layout or styling limitations, you can visually-hide the `<legend>` (ensuring it remains accessible), and visually replace it with a label that you can style any way you want:

```html
<!-- To work around <legend> styling limitations, you may visually hide the legend and replace it with a text label that is more styliable -->

<fieldset>
  <legend class="visually-hidden">
    [ fieldset label ]
  </legend>
  <span aria-hidden="true"
    >[ fieldset label ]</span
  >

  <!-- group controls -->
</fieldset>
```

Since the `<legend>` is only visually-hidden, it will be used as an accName for the `<fieldset>` and announced by screen readers as it normally would. So, remember to hide the visual label using `aria-hidden="true"` to avoid duplicate announcements.

## Further identifying input purpose

Labels and instructions are meant to help all users understand what input data is expected, and to help them provide that input with as little friction as possible.

In addition to labels and instructions required by SC 3.3.2, [SC 1.3.5 Identify Input Purpose (Level AA)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/quickref/#identify-input-purpose) states that:

> **The purpose of each input field collecting information about the user can be programmatically determined**

This criterion exists to provide even more _specific_ information about the input purpose **so that browsers and assistive technologies can present this purpose to users using different modalities.** To meet this requirement, you should **use the HTML autocomplete attribute.**

For some `<input>` fields, the type attribute already offers a way to **broadly** specify the intention of the field (for example, input `type="tel"`, input `type="email"`, or input `type="url"`).

However, type values are kind of like broad categories, describing the type of input, but not necessarily _its purpose_, especially as it relates to user-specific input fields.

For example, the `<input type="text">` can be used to collect different types of user input such as name and/or surname; home address, shipping address, billing address, and so on and so forth. And `<input type="email">` indicates that the field is for an e-mail address but does not clarify if the purpose is for entering the user’s e-mail address or some other person’s e-mail.

Currently, the `autocomplete` attribute is the only way of providing input purpose in HTML (but this might change in the future).

The `autocomplete` attribute provides information about the purpose of a form field, and this information can be used by browsers and assistive technologies to improve the user experience and, by extension, the accessibility and usability of forms:

1. Browsers and assistive technologies can customize the way they present this purpose to the user using different modalities to make it more understandable. For example, they may display familiar icons next to input fields to help users who have difficulties reading (like an icon of a birthday cake in front of an input field with `autocomplete="bday"`, or an icon of a telephone in front of an input field with `autocomplete="tel"`).

2. Browsers can offer to auto-fill some of the information requested in the form fields, which reduces the user’s need to manually input this information. This is particularly useful for people with motor impairments.

So the `autocomplete` attribute **“hints” to browsers and assistive technologies how they can improve the user experience.**

Even though some browsers might try to provide these auto-complete options for you, you can improve the results shown when you properly mark up the inputs with the appropriate `autocomplete` value.

```html
<body>
  <html>
    <input type="text" autocomplete="name" />
    <input
      type="text"
      autocomplete="family-name" />
    <input type="text" autocomplete="username" />

    <input
      type="text"
      autocomplete="address-line1" />
    <input
      type="text"
      autocomplete="address-line2" />

    <input type="email" autocomplete="email" />
  </html>
</body>
```

The HTML autocomplete attribute accepts one of 51 fixed values. You can find [a list of these values in the HTML specification](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/form-control-infrastructure.html#attr-fe-autocomplete) and in the [MDN autocomplete entry](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Attributes/autocomplete).

One of my favorite applications for the `autocomplete` attribute is text fields requiring a one-time verification code:

```html
<input
  type="text"
  inputmode="decimal"
  autocomplete="one-time-code" />
```

The `one-time-code` value of the `autocomplete` attribute enables the user to quickly autofill inputs with security codes sent by SMS. On iOS, Safari will display one-time codes in the QuickType bar so users can fill them with one tap.

## Make forms easier to use by specifying input mode

Similar to how the `autocomplete` attribute improves form usability, the `inputmode` attribute can make forms easier to use on mobile devices. And since we should always aim for usability beyond conformance, I think it’s worth dedicating a small section to this attribute.

The `inputmode` attribute is an enumerated attribute that **hints at the type of data that might be entered** by the user while editing the element or its contents. **This allows a browser to display an appropriate virtual keyboard.**

The `inputmode` attribute accepts one of [eight fixed values](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/interaction.html#attr-inputmode):

- none
- text
- tel
- url
- email
- numeric
- decimal
- search

You can see the different virtual keyboards provided by mobile browsers for the different inputmode values using a web app called [“Build a better mobile input”](https://better-mobile-inputs.netlify.app/?android=false&autocomplete=one-time-code&inputmode=decimal&type=email) by [Alex Holachek](https://github.com/aholachek).

![The Build A Better Mobile Input Web app. Screenshot.](/markdown-assets/31.12.inputmode-usage.png)

You can experiment with different combinations of `type` and `inputmode` attributes and see the virtual keyboard they produce on mobile devices. You can also see all the values for the `autocomplete` attribute and use those as well. The tool will also generate a code snippet that you can copy-paste into your work.

The `inputmode` attribute dramatically improves the user experience for users on mobile devices. But note that it does not enforce any input requirements on the user. It has no impact on the validity or invalidity of the fields it is used on.

## Outro

The accessibility and usability of forms can be dramatically improved by nothing more than ensuring that your fields are properly labelled, hints and instructions are provided where they are needed, and that these labels and instructions are properly associated with their controls. Groups of related controls should also be properly labelled to provide necessary context when it is needed.

Attributes such as autocomplete and inputmode further improve the accessibility and usability of forms. By using these attributes, you enable browsers and assistive technologies to further improve your users’ experience, and their ability to understand input requirements, as well as their ability to fill the required information in with as little friction as possible.

In an ideal world, when we do all of this on our end, users will know exactly what to fill in each form field, and they will do so without making mistakes. But there are many reasons why a user may either provide improperly-formatted information in a field, or leave one blank. When that happens, a programmatic error occurs, and we should help them understand not only what’s wrong, but also how to fix it. This is where form validation and error handling come in, which we’re going to take a deep dive into in the next chapter.
