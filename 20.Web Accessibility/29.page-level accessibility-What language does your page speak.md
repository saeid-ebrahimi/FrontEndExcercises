Have you ever tried to pronounce a word in a foreign language that you didn’t recognize? I bet you’ve sounded funny and maybe even got a good laugh out of it. If you try to speak a Spanish sentence using English pronunciation, for example, the words won’t come out right, and are likely going to be difficult to understand by someone who only speaks Spanish.

## Why is document language important?

Assistive technologies like screen readers and other Text-To-Speech (TTS) software speak page content out to the user. And they need to speak the content in its natural language so that it can be understood by their users. So they **need to know what language a page is in, in order to speak or display it properly.** They need to know whether they can go ahead and produce output from the text, or whether perhaps they need to switch to a different language mode.

Most screen readers can speak content in foreign languages, **as long as the content language is identified in the HTML.**

If the page’s language is not identified, screen readers will try to _guess_ the language of the page, and they will typically fall back to the user’s default system setting. For example if the user’s default system language is English, then unless the document language has been explicitly identified in the document or overridden in the browser, a screen reader will usually guess that the page content is set in English, which may not be correct. It will then speak the content using English pronunciation, potentially making it difficult to understand.

For example, here I have a page here that contains 4 sentences in 4 different languages: English, German, French, and Spanish. And then I have a list of numbers from 1 to 10. I have no language defined in the markup, so both NVDA on Windows and VoiceOver on macOS will default to English — which is my operating system’s default language, and will read all four sentences using English pronunciation. The numbers will also be read in English.
