# Implementing WCAG: Understanding and Techniques documents

The success criteria in WCAG are written in a way that may be open to interpretation, and there are often one or more ways a criterion can be satisfied. How do you know what technique(s) to use to meet a success criterion?

**Take SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)** for example. The criterion states that Information, structure, and relationships conveyed through presentation can be programmatically determined or are available in text.

What does that exactly mean in practice? How do you apply that in code? And how is it tested?

## WCAG supporting documents

To make meeting WCAG success criteria more attainable, supporting documents exist to:

1. help in the **understanding of the criteria**, and
2. provide practical steps that you can take, as well as recommendations that you can follow to satisfy the success criteria.

There are three main supporting documents for WCAG 2.1:

### [Understanding WCAG 2.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/)

![Understanding WCAG 2.1]("./markdown assets/Understanding WCAG.webp")

Understanding WCAG is, as the name suggests, **a guide to understanding and implementing the WCAG guidelines and success criteria**. In it, there is a short “Understanding” document for each guideline and success criterion in WCAG as well as key topics.

Each Understanding page includes more information about a criterion such as:

- the intent of the criterion (why does this success criterion exist),
- the benefits and how it helps people with disabilities (what barriers it addresses),
- real-world examples,
- test cases,
- links to techniques to implement the criterion,
- links to failures, and
- useful related resources.

To make finding the relevant information for each criterion easier, each success criterion in the WCAG standard includes a link to its corresponding Understanding page.

![Understanding WCAG]("./markdown assets/05.2.understanding-WCAG.webp")

The Understanding pages also include links to the relevant Techniques pages.

### [Techniques for WCAG 2.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Techniques)

[Techniques for WCAG 2.1]("./markdown assets/05.3.techniques from WCAG.webp")

The Techniques for WCAG document provides **a collection of techniques for satisfying the WCAG success criteria, as well as common failures**, each in a separate document that includes a description, examples, code, and tests.

As we mentioned earlier, each success criterion’s Understanding document includes links to the relevant techniques to satisfy the success criterion.

![Techniques for WCAG]("./markdown assets/05.4.Techniques for WCAG.webp")

### [How to Meet WCAG (Quick Reference)]("https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/quickref/")

You can find both the Understanding and the Techniques documents for each success criterion provided in a convenient checklist format in the “How to meet WCAG (Quick Reference)” document.

![How to Meet WCAG (Quick Reference)]("./markdown assets/05.5.How to Meet WCAG (Quick Reference).webp")

The WCAG Quick Reference is a key resource for designers and developers that you will most likely be using quite often in your accessibility work. It includes all of the guidelines, success criteria, and techniques and failures, **organized and customizable to make navigating and finding success criteria and their relevant supporting documents easier.**

The Quick Reference includes guidelines and criteria from WCAG 2.0 and WCAG 2.1, and can be filtered in many ways to help you focus on relevant content.

You can customize the Quick Reference to show what you are interested in at the time. There are “filters” for different roles (such as visual design, content creation, etc.), topics, and technologies (such as HTML, CSS, ARIA).

You can choose to display success criteria relevant to a specific type of content or design feature, such as animation, audio, forms, icons, language, and many more.

You can also choose to display only the success criteria relevant to the conformance level you’re aiming to achieve.

Under each success criterion is a list of sufficient techniques (that is, you can meet the success criterion if you implement those techniques), as well as advisory techniques, which are suggestions for ways to improve the accessibility of your work even further. It also lists common failures — things not to do otherwise you’d fail the success criterion.

If you need to understand a success criterion more, you can follow the link to the corresponding Understanding page, which is also available next to each criterion’s content.

## Getting from one document to another

The WCAG supporting documents are interlinked, which makes getting from one document to another easier.

![Getting from one document to another]("./markdown assets/05.6.Getting from one document to another.webp")

The WCAG standard contains links to Understanding pages as well as to the “How to meet” quick reference.

Each Understanding page includes links to the relevant techniques for each criterion.

The Quick Reference contains a list of all success criteria with links to corresponding Understanding pages, the relevant techniques for each criterion, as well as a link to the success criterion in the WCAG standard.

You can use the Quick Reference as a starting point for understanding and implementing the WCAG success criteria that you are aiming to satisfy in your project(s).

These documents are meant to take the guesswork out of accessibility. They exist to help you understand and apply the guidance provided in WCAG.

However, you are not required to follow the guidance and techniques provided in these documents to be conformant. You may do that, and you probably will (because they are helpful!); but you are not required to follow them because they are **non-normative** and, therefore, do not impact conformance claims.

So, what does non-normative exactly mean?

## Normative vs non-normative guidelines

Specifications and documentations can contain two types of information: **normative** and **non-normative**.
aria-required
Normative sections are the parts of the specification that are meant to be followed. They provide **requirements** that you must follow in order to be in conformance with the spec. They are prescriptive.

Normative sections are usually contrasted with non-normative sections.

Non-normative sections (sometimes called ‘informative’ or ‘advisory’) may include **supplemental information** useful to understanding the requirements in the normative sections, such as additional guidance, suggestions, recommendations, and tutorials, for example. Non-normative sections are **descriptive**. You do not have to follow them to be conformant with WCAG.

To say that content is conformant to WCAG (or that it conforms to WCAG) means that the content “conforms with the normative sections of WCAG”. The main content of WCAG 2.1 is normative and defines requirements that impact conformance claims.

The supporting documents contain examples of suggested or recommended practice, but it is not required to follow such recommendations in order to conform to the WCAG 2.1 specification.

This means that you may, for example, conform to a success criterion by implementing practices other than those provided in the advisory techniques.

For example, the description of [Success Criterion 3.3.3 Error Suggestion (Level AA) in the WCAG Quick Reference](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/quickref/?showtechniques=333#error-suggestion) includes a list of sufficient techniques that you can follow to meet this criterion. One of these techniques is **ARIA2: Identifying a required field with the `aria-required` property**.

While you could use the `aria-required` attribute to mark a native HTML field as required, you don’t have to use it because the native `HTML required` attribute has good support across browsers and screen readers. This is an example of where you can meet a success criterion without following the techniques listed in the reference. The Quick Reference includes a Note in the description that clearly states that "_Other techniques may also be sufficient if they meet the success criterion._"

This also emphasizes the fact that **a deep understanding of HTML and ARIA, as well as proper and frequent testing, is often more reliable than following a checklist of techniques.** Always consider WCAG and the supporting documents as a baseline or a starting point for your work, but make sure you do your own testing, even when you’re using age-old techniques, because browser and assistive technology support for features changes rapidly, and may often impact your work or how you implement an accessibility requirement.

All this being said, you are encouraged to consider the full range of techniques, including the advisory techniques, as well as to seek relevant advice about current best practice to ensure that Web content is accessible, as far as possible, to your users.

I encourage you to take some time to explore the WCAG Quick reference, browse through the criteria and learn more about each of them before moving on to the next chapter.
